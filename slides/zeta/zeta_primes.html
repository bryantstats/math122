Where we left off

\(p\)-series test. \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k^{p}}\) converges if \(p > 1\) and diverges if \(p \le 1\).

  • \(p=1\): the harmonic series \(1+\frac12+\frac13+\cdots\) diverges, even though its terms go to \(0\).
  • \(p=2\): \(1+\frac14+\frac19+\cdots\) converges. To what?

Today’s goal: follow this one simple formula to one of the deepest open problems in mathematics. Along the way we’ll discover

  1. a famous number hiding in \(\sum 1/k^2\),
  2. why the \(p\)-series knows everything about prime numbers,
  3. what the Riemann Hypothesis says, and why it’s worth $1,000,000.

A new function is born

The Basel problem: where does \(\pi\) come from?

  • 1650: Mengoli asks for the exact value of \(1+\frac14+\frac19+\frac1{16}+\cdots\)
  • The best mathematicians of Europe (including the Bernoulli brothers in Basel) fail.
  • 1734: a 27-year-old Leonhard Euler shocks everyone:

\[ 1+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{9}+\frac{1}{16}+\cdots \;=\; \frac{\pi^{2}}{6}. \]

A sum of fractions of whole numbers produces \(\pi\), the number of circles. Turn it around and compute \(\pi\) from the \(p\)-series:

The key that opens the door to primes

Every whole number \(> 1\) is a product of primes in exactly one way: \(\;360 = 2^3\cdot 3^2 \cdot 5,\;\; 1001 = 7\cdot 11\cdot 13,\;\; 97 = 97.\)

Now recall geometric series (\(|r|<1\)): \(\;1+r+r^2+r^3+\cdots = \dfrac{1}{1-r}\). Take \(r = \frac{1}{2^p}\) and \(r=\frac{1}{3^p}\):

\[ \underbrace{\Big(1+\tfrac{1}{2^p}+\tfrac{1}{4^p}+\tfrac{1}{8^p}+\cdots\Big)}_{=\;1/(1-2^{-p})} \underbrace{\Big(1+\tfrac{1}{3^p}+\tfrac{1}{9^p}+\cdots\Big)}_{=\;1/(1-3^{-p})} = 1+\tfrac{1}{2^p}+\tfrac{1}{3^p}+\tfrac{1}{4^p}+\tfrac{1}{6^p}+\tfrac{1}{8^p}+\tfrac{1}{9^p}+\tfrac{1}{12^p}+\cdots \]

Multiplying out gives each number built from 2s and 3s exactly once (unique factorization). Use every prime and you get every whole number:

Euler’s product formula (1737) \[ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{p}} \;=\; \prod_{\text{primes } q}\frac{1}{1-q^{-p}} \;=\;\frac{1}{1-2^{-p}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-3^{-p}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-5^{-p}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-7^{-p}}\cdots \]

Euler’s proof that primes never run out

Put \(p = 1\) in Euler’s formula: \[ \underbrace{1+\frac12+\frac13+\frac14+\cdots}_{\text{harmonic series} \;=\; \infty} \;=\; \frac{1}{1-\frac12}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\frac13}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\frac15}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\frac17}\cdots \]

If there were only finitely many primes, the right side would be a finite product of finite numbers. But the left side is the harmonic series, which diverges. Contradiction!

The divergence of \(\sum \frac1k\) (the \(p=1\) case) proves there are infinitely many primes.

How many primes are there? More than squares!

Compare two series with “sparse” denominators: \[ \sum \frac{1}{\text{squares}} = 1+\frac14+\frac19+\frac1{16}+\cdots = \frac{\pi^2}{6} \quad\textbf{converges} \qquad \sum \frac{1}{\text{primes}} = \frac12+\frac13+\frac15+\frac17+\frac1{11}+\cdots \quad\textbf{diverges!} \]

(Euler again.) So, in the sense of series, primes are more plentiful than perfect squares, but the prime sum grows incredibly slowly, like \(\ln(\ln x)\):

Bonus: \(\pi\) hiding in pairs of random numbers

Pick two random whole numbers. What’s the chance they share no common factor?

Chance they are not both divisible by 2 is \(1-\frac{1}{2^2}\); by 3, \(1-\frac{1}{3^2}\); by 5, \(1-\frac{1}{5^2}\); … Multiply over all primes, and Euler’s product flips upside down: \[ P(\text{no common factor}) = \prod_{q}\Big(1-\frac{1}{q^{2}}\Big) = \frac{1}{\zeta(2)} = \frac{6}{\pi^{2}} \approx 60.8\%. \]

Counting primes: Gauss’s discovery

Let \(\pi(x)\) = number of primes \(\le x\) (just a name, nothing to do with \(3.14\)).

At age 15 (≈1792), Gauss studied tables of primes and noticed that near \(x\), about 1 in every \(\ln x\) numbers is prime. That suggests \[ \pi(x) \approx \frac{x}{\ln x} \qquad\text{or, better,}\qquad \pi(x) \approx \operatorname{li}(x) = \int_0^x \frac{dt}{\ln t}\quad(\text{add up the densities}). \]

The Prime Number Theorem (1896)

\[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\pi(x)}{x/\ln x}=1.\] Proved 100 years after Gauss’s guess by Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin, using Riemann’s version of \(\zeta\).

\(x\) \(\pi(x)\) \(\operatorname{li}(x)\) \(\operatorname{li}(x)-\pi(x)\) \(\sqrt{x}\)
\(10^3\) 168 178 10 32
\(10^6\) 78,498 78,628 130 1,000
\(10^9\) 50,847,534 50,849,235 1,701 31,623
\(10^{18}\) 24,739,954,287,740,860 24,739,954,309,690,415 21,949,555 \(10^9\)

The error is tiny next to \(\pi(x)\): roughly \(\sqrt{x}\) in size. Is it always? That question turns out to be the Riemann Hypothesis.

Part II · Riemann (1859): what if \(p \le 1\)?

Bernhard Riemann wrote just one paper on primes: “On the number of primes less than a given magnitude” (1859). His idea:

Make sense of \(\zeta(p)\) for every \(p\), not just \(p>1\), and the primes will reveal their secrets.

But wait: for \(p\le 1\) the series \(\sum 1/k^p\) diverges. How can \(\zeta(p)\) have a value there?

Trick: use an alternating series. Flip every other sign: \[ \eta(p) = 1-\frac{1}{2^p}+\frac{1}{3^p}-\frac{1}{4^p}+\cdots \] By the alternating series test, this converges for every \(p>0\).

Rescuing \(\zeta\) with algebra

For \(p>1\), subtract the two series: \[ \begin{aligned} \zeta(p)-\eta(p) &= 2\Big(\frac{1}{2^p}+\frac{1}{4^p}+\frac{1}{6^p}+\cdots\Big) = \frac{2}{2^{p}}\Big(1+\frac{1}{2^p}+\frac{1}{3^p}+\cdots\Big) = 2^{1-p}\,\zeta(p)\\[4pt] \Longrightarrow\quad \zeta(p) &= \frac{\eta(p)}{1-2^{1-p}}. \end{aligned} \]

The right side makes sense for all \(p>0\) except \(p=1\). So we use it as the definition of \(\zeta(p)\) for \(0<p<1\).

About “\(1+2+3+\cdots=-\frac{1}{12}\)”: that series diverges (\(p=-1\)). What’s true is that Riemann’s extended function has \(\zeta(-1)=-\frac1{12}\).

Going 2D: inputs that are points

Riemann went further: he fed \(\zeta\) inputs that are points \((x,y)\) in the plane. (The official language is complex numbers, but we can do it with arrows.)

For the point \((x,y)\), the term \(\dfrac{1}{k^{p}}\) becomes an arrow of length \(\dfrac{1}{k^{x}}\) turned by angle \(y\ln k\).
\(\zeta(x,y)\) = where you land after placing all the arrows tip to tail.

  • When \(y=0\) the arrows all point the same way: it’s our old \(p\)-series with \(p=x\).
  • Total length of the path \(=\sum 1/k^{x}\), which is a \(p\)-series! It’s finite exactly when \(x>1\).
  • For \(0<x\le 1\) we use the alternating version \(\eta\) (flip every other arrow) and divide by \(1-2^{1-p}\) as before.

A zero of \(\zeta\) is a point \((x,y)\) where the arrow path ends exactly back at the start.

Riemann Hypothesis (1859). Apart from \(-2,-4,-6,\dots\), every zero of \(\zeta\) lies on the line \(x = \tfrac12\).

More than 10 trillion zeros have been checked: all on the line. No proof. Since 2000 it’s one of the Clay Institute’s $1,000,000 Millennium Prize Problems.

Walking up the line \(x=\tfrac12\)

On the critical line, the size of \(\zeta\) is given by a real function built from cosines and logs (Riemann–Siegel): \[ Z(y) \approx 2\sum_{k\le\sqrt{y/2\pi}} \frac{\cos\!\big(\theta(y) - y\ln k\big)}{\sqrt{k}}, \qquad \theta(y)\approx \frac{y}{2}\ln\frac{y}{2\pi}-\frac{y}{2}-\frac{\pi}{8}. \] Every time \(Z\) crosses zero, we’ve found a zero of \(\zeta\) on the line.

The music of the primes

Riemann’s explicit formula: the zeros build the primes. Take a staircase that steps up by \(\ln q\) at each prime \(q\) (and prime power): \[ \text{staircase}(x) = x \;-\; \sum_{\text{zeros } y} \frac{2\sqrt{x}\,\big(\tfrac12\cos(y\ln x) + y\sin(y\ln x)\big)}{\tfrac14+y^{2}} \;-\;\ln(2\pi)-\tfrac12\ln\!\big(1-x^{-2}\big) \] Each zero adds a wave: its height \(y\) sets the pitch, and \(\sqrt{x}\) (the \(\tfrac12\) in \(x=\tfrac12\)!) sets the volume.

Why the line \(x=\tfrac12\) matters

Each zero at \((x_0, y)\) adds a wave of size about \(\text{(input)}^{x_0}\) to the prime count.

  • All zeros on \(x_0=\tfrac12\) \(\Rightarrow\) every wave has size \(\approx \sqrt{x}\) \(\Rightarrow\) the error in counting primes is as small as it can be, like coin flips.
  • A single zero at \(x_0 = 0.7\) \(\Rightarrow\) a wave of size \(x^{0.7}\), much louder. The primes would be less regular than random.

\[\textbf{Riemann Hypothesis}\iff |\pi(x)-\operatorname{li}(x)| < \tfrac{1}{8\pi}\sqrt{x}\,\ln x \ \text{ for all } x\ge 2657.\] “The primes are as evenly spread as they possibly could be.”

Why care? Prime numbers secure every online purchase (RSA encryption), and hundreds of theorems begin with “Assuming the Riemann Hypothesis…”.

Breaking news (Oct 2026): an AI proves “quasi-RH”

Since 1896 we’ve known the nontrivial zeros sit in the strip \(0 < x < 1\). The quasi-Riemann Hypothesis: prove they sit in some narrower strip.

  • Oct 6, 2026: OpenAI released proofs written by an internal AI model:
    • 199 pages, checked by computer (Lean): zeros have \(\tfrac18 < x < \tfrac78\) (the stronger result)
    • a shorter 49-page proof of a slightly wider strip, \(\tfrac1{12} < x < \tfrac{11}{12}\), easier for humans to check
  • “the vast majority of consequences of RH already follow from quasi-RH” (M. Radziwiłł, NYU)
  • Still open: are the zeros exactly on \(x=\tfrac12\)? The $1M is unclaimed.
